What is an almost commuting differential operator?

Let $L$ be a linear ordinary differential operator (ODO) with the following shape:

\[L = \partial^n + u_2\partial^{n-2} + u_3\partial^{n-3} + \ldots + u_n,\]

where the coefficients $u_2,\ldots,u_n$ are differential variables (can be seen as functions in a variable $x$ such that $\partial(x) = 1$).

In general, when we compute the Lie bracket of two operators $A$ and $B$ or orders $n$ and $m$, respectively, we obtain a new operator of order $n+m-1$, measuring how much the operators $A$ and $B$ fail to commute. A central question in many applications is the study of the Centralizer of the operator $L$. Namely:

\[\mathcal{Z}(L) = \{P \ :\ [L,P] = LP - PL = 0\}.\]

However, studying the centralizer is not a trivial question. That is why we consider an intermediate step: the Almost Commuting Operators. We say that $Q$ almost-commutes with $L$ if their Lie bracket is "small" in some sense. More precisely, if the order of $[L,Q]$ is lesser than or equal to $n-2$.

The main property of the almost-commuting operator is that they form a $C$-vector space (where $C$ is the field of constants) and that we can compute a basis by taking monic operators for different orders.

Some considerations

  • The operator $L$ is monic: if not, we divide by this coefficient to make it monic.
  • The operator $L$ is in normal form (i.e., without a coefficient of order $n-1$): otherwise, there is a Gauge transformation (preserving the structure of the centralizer) that makes the operator in normal form.
  • The coefficients $u_i$ are differential variables.

Wilson's almost commuting basis

In his paper [1], G. Wilson describes the structure of a basis for almost-commuting differential operators for the generic operator $L$ of order $n$. This basis makes use of the idea of pseudo-differential operators (i.e., $C((\partial^{-1}))$).

Theorem. The centralizer of $L$ in the ring of pseudo-differential operators is a $C$-vector space generated by $L^{q/n}$ for $q \in \mathbb{Z}.

This Theorem talks about pseudo-differential operators. It is easy to see that $[L, L^{q/n}] = 0$. Let us split $L^{q/n} = A + B$ where

  • $A$ is a differential operator (also denoted by $A = (L^{q/n})_+$).
  • $B$ is a pseudo-differential operator with only negative powers of $\partial$ (also denoted by $B = (L^{q/n})_-$).

Then, we have the following:

\[0 = [L, L^{q/n}] = [L, A + B] = [L,A] + [L,B].\]

Since $B$ is a pseudo-differential operator with only negative powers of $\partial$, it follows that $[L,B]$ has order $n-2$. Hence, in order to cancel, the order of $[L,A]$ is lesser than or equal to $n-2$. Therefore, $A$ almost-commutes with $L$. This means we can compute the basis of almost commuting operators by computing the $n$-th root of $L$ as a pseudo-differential operator and then compute powers for every natural number.

In our paper [2], we developed a different approach to compute this basis. It follows a direct approach using only differential operators. Namely, let $Q = \partial^m + p_{2}\partial^{m-2} + \ldots + p_{m-1}\partial + p_m$, where the coefficients $p_i$ are unknown differential polynomials in the variables $u_j$ and their derivatives. Then, we impose the condition that the order of $[L,Q]$ is lesser than or equal to $n-2$. This gives us a set of differential equations on the coefficients $p_i$.

As shown in [2], this system is triangular ($p_i$ only depends on $p_j$ with $j\le i$) and we can write

\[\partial(p_i) = f(u_2,\ldots,u_n, p_2,\ldots,p_{i-1}),\]

where $f$ is a polynomial that can be symbolically integrated.
